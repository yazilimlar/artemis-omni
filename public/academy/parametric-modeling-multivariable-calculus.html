The Circle That Isn’t a Function

Parametric basics. A circle fails the vertical line test, so y = f(x) can never draw one. Give an angle t control of both coordinates at once — and the shape gains something new: motion.

Level · Simple

Say this

Presenter line“Parametric is motion, not just shape. The slider s is time — watch the dot travel the circle instead of the circle just sitting there.”

Paste into Desmos 2D

a = 2 (a·cos(t), a·sin(t)) point: (a·cos(s), a·sin(s)) ← press Play on s
Open Desmos 2D ↗
  • x = a·cos t and y = a·sin t — one input, two outputs. That’s the whole trick.
  • Same idea draws ellipses, spirals and orbits: just change the two recipes.
Calculus Hookr(t) = (a cos t, a sin t)  ·  r′(t) = (−a sin t, a cos t)
Velocity is always perpendicular to position: r · r′ = 0 for every t. Uniform circular motion, in one line — speed |r′| = a is constant.
x = a·cos(s) = —y = a·sin(s) = —speed is constant: the dot never hurries

Lissajous — Frequency Becomes Art

Let x wiggle p times while y wiggles q times. Change nothing but the ratio p : q and the same two sine waves weave knots, stars and bows.

Level · Simple

Say this

Presenter line“This is oscilloscopes, musical harmony and orbital resonance — one math. Integer ratios close the loop; messy ratios never quite repeat.”

Paste into Desmos 2D

p = 3 q = 4 d = 1.57 (sin(p·t), sin(q·t + d))
  • p : q = 1 : 1 with phase π/2 gives a circle — Example 1 was hiding inside this one.
  • The phase d twists the figure without changing its frequencies.
Calculus HookThe curve closes iff p/q ∈ ℚ. If p/q is irrational it never repeats — it comes arbitrarily close to every point in the square. Velocity is r′(t) = (p cos pt, q cos(qt+d)); it can vanish only when both cosines hit zero at the same t — for special (p, q, d) that is exactly where the figure develops a cusp or retraces itself (e.g. p = q = 1, d = 0 collapses to a line segment traced back and forth).
ratio p : q = 3 : 4closed curve — it retraces itself forever

The 3D Helix — The 2D → 3D Bridge

Take Example 1’s circle and let it climb while it turns. One parameter still drives everything, but the curve now lives in space: a spring, a screw thread, a DNA backbone, a spiral stair.

Level · Bridge to 3D

Say this

Presenter line“Nothing new was invented — we just gave the circle a third recipe, z = c·t. That is the whole jump from 2D curves to space curves.”

Paste into Desmos 3D / GeoGebra 3D

(R·cos t, R·sin t, c·t) t: 0 → 10π · R = radius · c = climb per radian
Open Desmos 3D ↗
  • R sets the coil radius; c sets the pitch — how fast it climbs per turn. Pitch = 2πc.
  • Project it flat (look down the axis) and you see Example 1’s circle again.
Calculus Hookr(t) = (R cos t, R sin t, c t) · r′(t) = (−R sin t, R cos t, c)
Speed |r′| = √(R² + c²) is constant — the tracer never speeds up. The helix is a geodesic on its cylinder (unroll the cylinder and it becomes a straight line) and the model of uniform motion on a screw. Curvature κ = R/(R²+c²), torsion τ = c/(R²+c²) — both constant.

Bézier Curve — This Is Parametric Modeling

Four points. One formula. Drag the orange control points and watch a perfectly smooth curve follow — this exact math draws every letter you’re reading, every car body, wing and CAD spline.

Level · Core idea

Say this

Presenter line“You never move the curve. You move two handles, and the math guarantees smoothness. Every modern design tool is this idea, scaled up.”

Paste into Desmos 2D — then drag A, B, C, D

A=(-4,-2) B=(-2,3) C=(3,3) D=(4,-1) (1-t)³A + 3(1-t)²t·B + 3(1-t)t²·C + t³D with 0 ≤ t ≤ 1
  • The weights (1−t)³, 3(1−t)²t, 3(1−t)t², t³ always sum to 1 — the point is a moving weighted average.
  • The faint lines show de Casteljau’s construction: averages of averages of averages.
  • Chain Béziers end-to-end and you get fonts, logos, animation paths, Revit splines.
Calculus HookThe Bernstein weights are a partition of unity: (1−t)³+3(1−t)²t+3(1−t)t²+t³ = ((1−t)+t)³ = 1. The derivative is itself a Bézier curve of the differences: B′(t) = 3[(1−t)²(B−A)+2(1−t)t(C−B)+t²(D−C)] — so endpoints are tangent to AB and CD.
↖ Drag A · B · C · D directly on the graph
point at t: —curve always stays inside the control points’ hull

Cycloid — The Rolling Wheel & the Fastest Slide

Fix a dot to the rim of a rolling wheel. It traces neither circle nor line — it invents a new curve. Flip that curve over and it becomes the brachistochrone: the fastest possible slide, beating a straight line while being longer.

Level · Medium

Say this

Presenter line“At the bottom of each arch the dot is momentarily stopped — that’s why the cusps are sharp. Galileo, Bernoulli and Newton all fought over this curve.”

Paste into Desmos 2D

(t − sin(t), 1 − cos(t)) rolling circle: center (s, 1), radius 1
  • One arch spans exactly 2πr wide and 2r tall — and its arc length is exactly 8r, a clean whole number from a rolling circle.
  • In the race, both beads drop the same height. The cycloid dives steeply early, banks speed, and wins.
Calculus HookSpeed |r′(t)| = 2|sin(t/2)| — exactly 0 at each cusp, maximum at the top of the arch. Arc length of one arch is ∫₀²π 2|sin(t/2)| dt = 8r. The inverted cycloid also solves the calculus-of-variations problem: it minimises descent time (brachistochrone) and is tautochrone — descent time is independent of starting point.
Watch the red dot: it stops dead at each cusp.
tracing point: —one arch = 2π wide, 2 tall (r = 1)

The Mountain — Your First Multivariable Function

Two inputs, one output: the graph stops being a line and becomes a landscape. Slice it with a flat plane at height k and the intersection is a contour line. Drag to orbit; the arrow is the gradient — straight uphill, always perpendicular to the contour.

Level · New dimension

Say this

Presenter line“A topographic map is just this surface, sliced. And that’s why water flows across contour lines, never along them — it follows the gradient, downhill.”

Paste into GeoGebra 3D

f(x,y) = 3·exp(−x² − y²) + 2·exp(−(x−2)² − (y−2)²) Plane: z = k (slider k, 0 to 3)
Open GeoGebra 3D ↗Open Desmos 3D ↗
  • Level curve: all (x, y) where f(x, y) = k — one flat slice of the mountain.
  • Gradient ∇f at the marker: direction of steepest climb, length = steepness.
  • Move the marker — near a peak the arrow shrinks to nothing. Peaks are where ∇f = 0.
Calculus Hook∇f ⊥ level sets. Proof in one line: along a level curve r(t) with f(r(t)) = k, differentiating gives ∇f · r′ = 0. So ∇f is normal to the contour, points in the direction of steepest ascent, and |∇f| is that steepest rate. The directional derivative is Duf = ∇f · u.

Slicing the Mountain — Partial Derivatives

A partial derivative is not mysterious. Freeze one variable, slice the mountain, and take an ordinary 1D slope on that slice. Do it in x and in y, and you know the full tilt of the surface at a point.

Level · Medium

Say this

Presenter line“∂f/∂x answers one question: if I walk purely east, how steep is it right here? ∂f/∂y asks the same walking north. Two honest 1D questions.”

Paste into GeoGebra 3D (continue from #6)

g(x) = f(x, b) ← slice, y frozen at b h(y) = f(a, y) ← slice, x frozen at a ∂f/∂x = slope of g at a ∂f/∂y = slope of h at b
  • The teal curve is the east–west slice; the orange curve is north–south. Their tangent lines are drawn at the marker.
  • Together the two slopes define the tangent plane — the flat sheet that just kisses the surface.
  • Gradient check: ∇f = ( ∂f/∂x , ∂f/∂y ) — exactly the arrow from Example 6.
Calculus HookThe two partials are the gradient: ∇f = (fx, fy), and they define the tangent plane z = f(a,b) + fx(a,b)(x−a) + fy(a,b)(y−b) — the best linear approximation, with error o(√((x−a)²+(y−b)²)) when f is differentiable.
Go deeper · Monkey Saddlez = x³ − 3xy²,  ∇f = (3x²−3y², −6xy), so ∇f(0,0) = (0,0) — yet the Hessian there is the zero matrix (degenerate). The surface has three valleys and three ridges (room for a monkey’s two legs and tail). The second-derivative test cannot classify this critical point — a failure mode with no 1D analogue.

Torus & Möbius — Parametric Surfaces

A curve needed one parameter. A skin needs two — u and v, like latitude and longitude, or the warp and weft of fabric. Every 3D model, game character and architectural shell is stored exactly this way.

Level · Advanced

Say this

Presenter line“Switch to the Möbius strip and follow the travelling dot. It goes all the way around u… and arrives back upside-down. One trip proves the strip has only one side.”

Paste into Desmos 3D / GeoGebra 3D

TORUS: ((2+0.7cos v)cos u, (2+0.7cos v)sin u, 0.7 sin v) MÖBIUS: ((1+ v/2·cos(u/2))cos u, (1+ v/2·cos(u/2))sin u, v/2·sin(u/2)) u: 0→2π · torus v: 0→2π · möbius v: −1→1
Open Desmos 3D ↗
  • Torus: u goes around the donut, v goes around the tube. Two circles, multiplied.
  • Möbius: the half-twist cos(u/2) is the entire trick — one half-turn baked into the formula.
  • Game engines call this grid a mesh; CAD calls it a parametric surface. Same object.
Calculus HookSurface area is ∬ |ru × rv| du dv. For the torus |ru × rv| = r(R + r cos v), so area = ∫₀²π∫₀²π r(R+r cos v) du dv = 4π²Rr and volume = 2π²Rr². The Möbius strip is non-orientable: no continuous unit normal exists over it, so flux ∬ F·n dS is not defined until you cut it.
Go deeper · Helicoid & Torus Knot (2,3)Helicoid: r(u,v) = (u cos v, u sin v, c v) — a ruled minimal surface (mean curvature H = 0), the soap-film shape on a helical frame. Torus knot (2,3): compose the torus with (u,v) = (2t, 3t) to get a single curve that winds twice around the hole and three times through it — a surface becoming a curve by composition.

Frenet-Swept Pipe — What CAD Actually Computes

The modeling finale. Take the helix from Example 3 as a spine, attach a moving frame to it, and sweep a small circle along it. The result is a solid pipe — a duct run, a thread, a handrail.

Level · Modeling finale

Say this

Presenter line“Curve + frame + profile = solid. This is not a picture of a pipe — it is the same boundary representation a CAD kernel builds when you route a duct or a pipe in Revit.”

Paste into GeoGebra 3D

r(t) = (R cos t, R sin t, c t) T = r′/|r′| · N = T′/|T′| · B = T × N P(t,θ) = r(t) + ρ(cos θ·N + sin θ·B) t: 0 → 4π · θ: 0 → 2π · ρ = pipe radius
Open GeoGebra 3D ↗
  • The bright ring is the profile circle at the travelling point — watch it stay perpendicular to the spine (that’s the Frenet frame doing its job).
  • Change ρ and you change wall thickness of the sweep, not the path.
Calculus HookFor the helix, with s = √(R²+c²): T = (−R sin t, R cos t, c)/s, N = (−cos t, −sin t, 0), B = T × N. The swept surface is P(t,θ) = r(t) + ρ(cos θ N + sin θ B) — a parametric surface whose two knobs are along (t) and around (θ). This curve + frame + profile construction is the B-rep a CAD kernel computes — behind every pipe, duct, thread and digital twin.

Finale — Volume Under a Surface, Then the Butterfly

First the payoff of multivariable calculus: fill a dome with thin columns — base dx·dy, height f(x, y) — and add them all. That sum is the double integral. Then, one parameter and one line of math… a butterfly.

Level · Finale

Say this

Presenter line“A 1D integral adds rectangles to get area. A double integral adds columns to get volume. Same idea, one dimension up — refine the grid and watch the estimate lock onto the exact answer.”

Paste into GeoGebra 3D / Desmos

VOLUME: f(x,y) = 4 − x² − y² over x² + y² ≤ 1 ∬ f dA = 7π/2 ≈ 10.996 BUTTERFLY (Desmos 2D, 0 ≤ t ≤ 24π): r = e^{sin t} − 2cos(4t) + sin⁵((2t−π)/24) (r·sin t, r·cos t)
  • Grid slider = the limit process, live: more, thinner columns → sum → exact value.
  • The butterfly (Temple Fay’s curve) is compression: a one-line rule generating a shape no one could plot point-by-point.
Calculus HookThe column sum is a Riemann sum: Σ f(xi,yj) ΔA → ∬D f dA as n → ∞ (Fubini lets you compute it as iterated 1D integrals). Here ∬ (4−x²−y²) dA = 7π/2 over the unit disk — in polar coordinates the Jacobian r dr dθ does the heavy lifting. For the butterfly, arc length has no elementary closed form, yet the parametric integral still converges.

The memory hook — end on this

If they remember five symbols, they remember the subject.

1 parameter →a curve, with motion built in
f(x, y) →a surface — a landscape over two inputs
2 parameters →a skin: torus, Möbius, every 3D model
∂ →slope of the surface, one frozen direction at a time
∬ →volume under the surface, column by column

Parametric equations are compression: simple rules, turned by one or two knobs, generating shapes — circles, slides, mountains, donuts, butterflies — that no static formula could draw. That is the whole subject. Everything else is turning the knobs with more care.
